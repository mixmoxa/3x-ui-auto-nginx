WORKSPACE / READING

Write decisions that can be revisited.

A decision note is useful when it preserves the reason behind a choice, particularly after the people involved have moved on.

NOTE 01

State the decision first

Begin with one sentence describing the chosen approach in the present tense. Follow it with the problem it addresses and the constraint that made the choice necessary. A future reader should not need to reconstruct a meeting from scattered messages. Distinguish a decision already in use from a proposal still being discussed. The difference determines whether someone can rely on the note while changing an interface or reviewing a deployment.

NOTE 02

Record the alternatives fairly

Keep at least one realistic alternative and explain why it was not selected. Avoid caricatures such as “the other option is complicated” unless you can identify the actual complexity. An alternative might have required a migration, changed a public URL or introduced a new operational dependency. If both approaches were acceptable, say why one was preferred under current conditions. Decisions often become difficult to understand when their trade-offs are rewritten as universal rules.

NOTE 03

Separate evidence from expectation

A small test, a documented browser behaviour and an informed estimate are different kinds of support. Label them. If the decision depends on a benchmark, preserve the environment and the quantity that was measured. If no measurement exists, do not invent one to make the document look complete. A reader can still act on a reasoned choice when the assumptions are explicit. The note becomes more useful when it includes a way to tell if the assumption has stopped holding.

NOTE 04

Define a review trigger

Good decisions do not require a ceremonial review every month. They require an observable reason to reconsider: a broken compatibility promise, a new requirement or recurring support failures. Write that trigger next to the conclusion. Link the implementation location where possible, then keep the note small. Changing the decision later should not erase the earlier argument. A short amendment explaining what changed makes the history easier to trust.

Further reading

Continue with the guides index, or explore the overview for the wider context. Try the method on a small unfinished task before using it for a larger project. Keep the original explanation next to the revised one, and record which decision became clearer. A useful note should remain understandable to someone who was not present for the discussion.
